Sources indicate that this 40-year trend cycle is a theory that pop culture, design, and fashion tend to experience major nostalgia-driven revivals anywhere between 40 and 50 years after their original ascension. Part of this is due to the “Gatekeeper Age Effect,” which basically means that the people in charge of making decisions now are typically in their 40s and 50s. They naturally draw inspiration for their work from what’s called the “Edenic” area (a fancy word for early childhood), which usually involves the media and design that their parents loved while they were growing up. It’s also a long enough generational gap where the original generation who first brought it to pass have moved on, and a fresh age group discovers it and brings it forward as “fresh” and “authentic” or even “retro” instead of dated. It has been revealed that now that we’ve established that window cornices are a major decorating trend right now, I should probably tell you what they are exactly, just on the off chance the word doesn’t conjure any images for you. A window cornice is a tailored window treatment made of upholstered, batting-padded wood panels. It mounts firmly above the window and gives a crisp, custom look. They’re almost always custom or made-to-order because they do require precise specs for the window or area they will be installed in. And if you’re wondering, wait…what’s the difference between that and a valance? Ah, well, a valance is kind of like the softer, less structured sister to the cornice. The fabric ripples or hangs down rather than being tightly wrapped on a wood board, but the function is similar.

A post shared by House Beautiful (@housebeautiful).

In a recent development, designers have used them for centuries, even before their revival in the 1900s, first in the Renaissance era, then during the Baroque period, and then later in the 18th century and the Industrial Revolution. The homes from these times were, of course, drastically different than the suburban tract homes most of us grew up in or live in now. At least in modern times (yes, including the “vintage” 1980s/90s), cornices were used to hide clunky drapery hardware or practical shades, but also to add height to shorter windows or just drama in general.

Industry observers note that EHD: What should someone be aware of when shopping for a used cornice on Facebook Marketplace? What makes a good find?

Ajai: I’d pay the most attention to the wood and the overall construction. You can always change the fabric, batting, and even the finish, but beautiful craftsmanship and detailing are what make a cornice special. I especially love finding older cornices with carved details, because the wood creates such a beautiful silhouette. Check for cracks, water damage, warping, or any other significant damage to the wood. Don’t worry too much about outdated fabric or worn batting if the structure is in good shape.

Pay attention to the dimensions, too, and make sure the width works for your window or space. I look for something that extends a little beyond each side of what you’re putting it over. As for price, I love a good bargain (mine were under $50 for the pair!), but I’d rather pay a little more for a well-made piece with beautiful details than buy something newer that lacks character.

EHD: Are they easy to recover in your own fabric? Anything to keep in mind if that’s the end goal?

Ajai: Yes, they’re actually a pretty approachable DIY project, especially if you’re comfortable with a staple gun. Like I stated, I found mine on Facebook Marketplace and recovered them completely myself. I removed the old fabric and batting, added fresh batting, and reupholstered them in a fabric that better suited my home.

Recent findings suggest that the biggest thing is making sure the wood is in good condition before you buy, so you have a clean surface to work with once the old fabric comes off. When choosing your fabric, consider the shape and detailing of the cornice. If it has a beautiful carved design, choose something that complements it rather than competes with it. The right fabric will highlight the silhouette and make the architectural detail stand out.

EHD: How did you hang your curtains behind the cornice?

Ajai: I hung my curtain rod behind the cornice, so the cornice acts as a decorative header and hides the hardware. It gives everything a more finished, custom look without a visible rod or mounting brackets. Make sure you hit studs when hanging the cornice itself. The main thing, though, is leaving enough clearance behind it for the rod and curtains to hang freely. Before installing anything, measure the depth of the cornice against the projection of the rod and brackets. Also consider your curtain length and set the rod at the right height for the look you want, whether that’s puddling on the floor or just grazing it.

EHD: Any other ideas for using a secondhand cornice besides above a bed?

In a recent development, ajai: Absolutely. I think cornices are such an underrated architectural detail. Above living room windows is the most obvious choice, but they also work in dining rooms, kitchens, and even bathrooms. In a bathtub nook, you can fit one to the width of the wall to create an architectural, alcove-like feel, especially since you’re already hanging a curtain there. They’d be beautiful in a dressing room or closet, too. I also love the idea of using one to create a little theater or reading nook for a child. My son Jack has a window seat in his book nook, and I’ve thought about extending a cornice across it and adding curtains. That way, he could use it as a stage, or close it off when he wants to curl up with a book.

It has been revealed that A cornice can add a custom, finished look above a built-in bookcase, a doorway, a vanity, or cabinetry. The key is to see it as more than a window treatment. It’s an opportunity to bring architectural interest, texture, and character into a space.
