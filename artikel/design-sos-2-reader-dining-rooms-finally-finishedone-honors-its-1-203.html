Recent findings suggest that you loved the last Design SOS so much, SURPRISE! We’ve got another one for you today. We normally only do these once a month or so, but in honor of the upcoming Thanksgiving holiday here in the States, we decided to do yet another post in this series, this time with two reader dining rooms! And boy, did I have fun with these. As soon as I saw the rich wood paneling of the first space (nope, she’s not painting it—nor do I think she should), and then read the “Give me all the patterns, please!” email of the other, I cracked my knuckles dramatically in the air and got to work. Let me not prolong this, because we’ve all got work to finish up before commencing celebrations…or work to procrastinate on while virtual sale-hopping, am I right??

From the reader: “I want to brighten up and modernize our heritage dining room…without painting the wood. I want this room to feel current, but heritage-inspired; I want to pay homage to the beautiful woodwork without feeling like we’re drowning in old-timey heritage! I would consider painting the wood, but my husband is very against this. The wood is in very good shape, and painting is hard to undo, so I understand his perspective and agree it makes sense to make some initial efforts without paint. I just don’t know what those could be! Please help! (Related: Breaking: Current Obsessions: Après Feast...)

Industry observers note that re: furniture. The mid-century modern table, four chairs, credenza, and detachable hutch all came with us from our previous home. It was the same era (1914), but the wainscoting was painted white, and the walls were a dark and stormy blue-grey. I love how this furniture looked in there. The hutch should go since we no longer have a wall to accommodate the credenza+hutch. I worry the credenza and table/chairs add more wood to the already wood-heavy room, but I like them and would love to keep them if reasonable.” — Lesley B. (Related: Industry News: I Replaced My 2015 Kindle with the New A...)

Some details the reader shared with me:.

In a recent development, those ceilings! That molding!! THAT BUILT-IN! Lesley, you’ve got yourself an absolutely stunning home if this dining room is any indication. I love that you want to honor the heritage of its 1914 pedigree, but I can understand how you want to make work some of the large furniture pieces you already have (and like) but also get a little playful. I want you to know upfront that I love Craftsman homes. I lived for three years on a street near Pasadena that was dotted with original 1900s Craftsman homes, and I admired each and every one of them every day on my neighborhood strolls. I dreamed of one of my neighbors asking me for decorating advice, and while I never got that, I’m so glad you emailed us, because I’m READY!

Industry observers note that now, serious yet playful, neutral with pops of muted colors, eclectic but craftsman but mid-century modern…I’ve got you, girl. Here’s what I’d do: Those white walls above the paneling are absolutely screaming for wallpaper. Since the woodwork is very stern, with all its 90-degree angles and vertical lines, we need something up here that is a bit more free-flowing. Given the period of the home (which coincides with American Arts & Crafts), a botanical or vine-like William Morris print would be beautiful. It doesn’t have to be quite that specific, but something in that world.

From there, here’s my hit list:.

Wallpaper | Rug | Chandelier | Chair Velvet | Lamps | Curtains | Squiggle Candelabra | Silver Trinket Box | Bowl Centerpiece | Vase Centerpiece | Dog Art | Floral Art | Curtain Rod.

Industry observers note that here is what I ended up doing! And you guys, I love it. Like, I want to move in here and make this my own dining room. I tried out some William Morris designs, but they all read too heavy, considering this reader wanted to brighten and modernize. This honeysuckle design from Scalamandré pays a subtle homage to a period-appropriate motif while feeling somewhat more current. I decided to leave it off the ceiling, but it would also work beautifully between the coffers, as well.

In the rug, I went with a dark ochre color because it’s similar in tone to the lighter mid-century wood of the table, chairs, credenza, and even the piano (which I originally thought to get out of there, but honestly, I don’t really mind it once everything is tidied up. Plus, if it’s in here, I have to imagine there isn’t room for it anywhere else). This works to add texture, warmth, and personality without bringing in too much tension. Low contrast is key. Plus, this color brings out the golden hues in the warm wood and is absolutely gorgeous in person!

Industry observers note that the chairs pick up the blue from the border detail; this should be a very easy upholstery job. I had these same chairs in my previous breakfast nook, and it just took a staple gun and about an hour to pull off. I love this vintage velvet, which doesn’t feel overly modern. Up top, the chandelier speaks to a few different styles, but the wood ball ties back to the paneling nicely.

Since she mentioned playful, I got squiggly with a ribbon-like candelabra on the credenza, and a wavy vase and large-scale bowl with loops as the dining room centerpieces. You can’t see it that well because it’s tiny, but there’s a small silver trinket box above a stack of coffee table books by the window. I liked the juxtaposition of the silver against all the wood, but also the linen fabric of the table lamp duo I picked. If we’re going to do contrast, we can do it in more subtle ways, like this.

It has been revealed that here’s a rendering of the room to get a sense of what it would feel like. The rug color got a little muted, but in general, it came out pretty good, I think! I know she had a lot more art on the ledge throughout the room; they were hard to add into the design, but one way to make them feel more purposeful, as I mentioned, is to reframe them. I’d pick a mix of light wood, white, and antique gilt frames, and maybe even add some mats to some of them to elevate their look (a linen or even color fabric mat on one or two would be killer).

In terms of costs, we’re flying high, people! It was hard for me to calculate the appropriate number of wallpaper rolls needed, but some quick math landed me at seven rolls (please have an expert check this and give you an accurate estimate!). That alone is $1,323 at the sale price thanks to Black Friday; this doesn’t include installation costs, of course. Once we add in everything else, including four curtain panels, the total comes to a little over $3,700 (before applicable taxes and shipping).

Once I realized I was well below her $5,000 to $10,000 budget, I decided to take a look back at the image, and that’s when I recalled that she only had four proper dining chairs. The other two chairs at the table were high chairs/toddler chairs, so I decided to add in two head chairs for a more finished look. If it were my room, I’d probably move the kid chairs into one of the corners (after tidying up the clutter in here), and just set them there in waiting, out of the way. When dinner came around in this formal space, I’d shift a few things and make room for them. I have those same Stokke chairs, btw, and they are easy to slide into an open corner of a table like this, even with six adults already seated.

It has been revealed that with the two new head chairs (in a delicious mustard velvet, which, if you know anything about me and my design tendencies, is a hard color and fabric combination for me to avoid, especially when rich wood tones are in play), the total increased by $1,015, which brings us to just south of $4,800. That leaves plenty of room in the budget for price fluctuations—especially if she’s not ready to buy now with existing Black Friday prices—more art, frames and decor if desired, upholstery and wallpaper install services, and even a new custom Roman shade if the existing one needs an upgrade once everything else is in place. Those are expensive, but we’re still looking at well under the top of her $10,000 allotment.

I hope where I landed with my design indicates that I was trying to honor the home’s architecture and period, the reader’s affinity for clean, serious yet playful aesthetics, and make the mid-century furniture that was inherited from their previous home work.

Now, let’s move on to Design SOS case #2….

In a recent development, from the reader: “I’m hoping to get some guidance on how to tackle all the textiles in our dining room, which is right off the kitchen. I’ve been reading the blog for years and have learned so much from you ladies (Thank you!!), but I’m having analysis paralysis at this point. I really love the direction this room is heading, with a mix of primitive vintage finds and more modern accents. I recently painted the walls and ceiling in Behr’s Flint Smoke, and I’m really happy with it. But now I’m feeling stuck on the finishing touches, specifically curtains, a rug, and reupholstering the dining chairs.

According to recent reports, I absolutely love the look of mixed patterns and textures, but I haven’t quite figured out how to pull it off myself! Nearly all the furniture in this room was scored on Facebook Marketplace for great prices, so I’m finding it especially hard to commit to the finishing pieces that I’ll likely need to purchase new. For reference, we’d need an 8×10 rug to fit under the dining table and curtains around 95 inches long. I’m also happy to tackle reupholstering the chair seats myself, so there’s definitely room to have some fun with fabric! I know rugs and curtains are pricey, but I’m willing to invest, especially since the furniture was so affordable.” — Liz F.

Some details the reader shared with me:.

Finding so much of her furniture on Facebook Marketplace is right up my alley, Liz! I love that you were able to save so much money, and what great pieces! Those chairs and the table are killer, and with the right styling, that credenza is going to look fantastic. I love when a space feels collected, and this is definitely where you’re headed. But, as this reader mentioned, it needs that next layer, and that’s where I come in. I wanted to know more about what she was after, because tastes in patterns vary greatly. I wish I could show you what Liz sent me (they were so good and just my style!!!), but due to photo usage rights, I can’t. However, what I pulled from the set of images she shared with me was the following: Each of them had stripes, windowpane plaid, gingham, petite florals, and some quilt/primitive patterns. Some leaned more contemporary than others, but in general, those were the prints she was drawn to. As for colors, she noted to me in a separate line of communication that she’s a blues, greens, and browns gal, so I’ll honor that.

Recent findings suggest that and here is where I landed! The photos of this room were quite dark, which makes it hard to get a realistic rendering in the end. Plus, I didn’t think you could really appreciate the patterns I put together (especially since I couldn’t add in the chair fabric with any success). So I decided to put together a moodboard for you and the reader to see better, and I think it tells a much more cohesive yet eclectic story than the above:.

Rug | Curtain Panels | Roman Shade | Chair Upholstery Fabric | Velvet Chair Trim | Existing Paint Color | Mirror | Table Lamp w/ Lampshade | Blue Bowl | Taper Candle Holders.

According to recent reports, the most consistent pattern from Liz’s inspiration photos was a windowpane grid, so I decided to go significant with that with a rug. And this one came in at under $800 at All Modern (it’s even cheaper—$549 at another site called Incredible Rugs and Decor, but I’ve never shopped from there so not sure how reputable it is! It’s wool, low-pile, and echoes the wall color a bit with some added depth. From there, I moved into the chair upholstery. This one took me a while because I wanted it to honor her obvious love of stripes (again, from her design inspiration she emailed me) but still layer in a small additional print, given we didn’t have a ton of other surfaces to work with, unlike on a sofa or bed where pillows can do heavy lifting in the world of pattern mixing. I finally found this one on Spoonflower, and I love it! She’ll have to pick an upholstery-grade fabric (I like the performance linen for here). The chairs have a trim, so I went with a contrasting deep burgundy, which I then carried through the lamp shade from Pooky that adds in some gingham-like spirit.

Recent findings suggest that next was that window, which I was itching to throw a Roman shade on! This one is from Heidi Caillier’s collab with Lulu & Georgia. In truth, I do not know the size of Liz’s window, so this may not fit, but I hope it gives her enough inspiration to find something that does. Since I did a small, tight floral in the shade, I lean into another grid on the panels, though if you look closely, these have a very subtle vine-like botanical detail that makes up the squares! I had to make these pieces do double and triple duty! A new mirror that showcases its own geometric frame, ribbed candle holders for texture (and more visual interest), and a gorgeous painterly centerpiece bowl round it all out.

In terms of budget, this all comes in at…wait for it…just over $2,000, including 4 curtain panels. The chairs are a box cushion with cording, which is way beyond my ability to DIY, so if this reader is able, as she mentioned she may be, great! If not, she’ll be looking at probably another $500-$1,000 for re-upholstery, depending on where she lives and who she hires.

Liz, I hope this is close to what you were hoping, or at the very least, inspires you to make it yours, using this as a jumping-off point!

Regarding “Clean up the clutter! ” The clutter is there for a reason and it will stay because kids play daily, have art supplies, homework, etc. The only thing to help it is to incorporate newly introduced furniture and reorganize things, such that it’s easier to keep things out of sight. It’s impossible to throw away books, art supplies, and so on. And it’s there because no other place at home can hold this at this moment. One of the problems with modern-day layouts is that it’s very sterile. It relies on closets and invisible furniture to hold everything that’s needed. Rooms are awkward, with too many exits or too small to fit the furniture that’s needed. The inspirations are beautiful but those houses probably have separate craft rooms, breakfast nooks, piano rooms, libraries. The challenge isn’t just to make the room beautiful, but to make it multipurpose with adequate storage that fits a modern-day family life indoors.
